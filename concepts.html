Core Health & PE concepts

These four ideas show up again and again this year — in 3.1, 3.3, 3.4, and the exam. They're not just theory: they're the lens you use to explain why something affects wellbeing, not just that it does. Getting comfortable with these now will make every assessment easier.

Hauora

Hauora is a Māori view of wellbeing, often explained through Te Whare Tapa Whā — picture a wharenui (meeting house) with four walls, all needed to keep the whole building standing:

Taha tinana (physical wellbeing), taha hinengaro (mental and emotional wellbeing), taha whānau (social wellbeing, your relationships and sense of belonging), and taha wairua (spiritual wellbeing, your sense of identity, purpose, or connection to something bigger than yourself).

When you're analysing an issue, ask: which of these four dimensions does it affect, and how? Strong answers usually touch on more than one.

Socio-ecological perspective

This is the idea that wellbeing isn't just about individual choices — it's shaped by the layers of environment someone lives in: their whānau and friends, their school or workplace, their community, and wider society (laws, policies, media, culture, economics).

Instead of asking "why did this person make that choice?", a socio-ecological lens asks "what wider conditions made this choice more or less likely, or more or less available to them?" It's a key way to move an answer beyond blaming individuals and toward understanding systems.

Health promotion

Health promotion is about improving wellbeing at a population level, not just treating individuals. It's often linked to the Ottawa Charter, which outlines action areas like building healthy public policy, creating supportive environments, strengthening community action, developing personal skills, and reorienting health services.

When an assessment asks about a "strategy" or "intervention," it's usually asking you to think at this bigger-picture level — what could change the environment or system, not just tell individuals to try harder.

Attitudes and values

Values are the beliefs and principles that matter to a person or group — things like fairness, autonomy, tradition, or care for others. Attitudes are the positions people take as a result of those values.

Different people can look at the exact same issue and land in very different places, because they're starting from different values. Being able to explain why a group holds a perspective (not just what they believe) usually means naming the value or belief underneath it.
