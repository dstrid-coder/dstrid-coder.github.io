Why HPE?

In Year 9 and 10, Health and PE run together as one subject — building your foundation in hauora, movement, and understanding what shapes people's participation in physical activity, through hands-on units and practical experience.

From Year 11, Health and PE split into two separate subjects that you can take individually or together:

  • Physical Education combines practical performance with theory — biophysical principles, sports psychology, safety management, and leadership, applied through real physical activity and sport.
  • Health is more theory-based — analysing health issues, evaluating health practices, and thinking critically about ethical issues, wellbeing, and society.

You can take one, the other, or both — they cover different ground and are assessed differently.

Do I have to be good at sport to do well in PE?

No. PE assesses effort, understanding, and improvement — not natural talent. Much of the assessment is about how well you understand and apply concepts like biomechanics or safety management, and how much you improve, not how skilled you already are.

Is Health just sitting in a classroom?

Mostly theory-based, yes, but it's discussion and research-heavy rather than lecture-heavy — you'll be analysing real issues, debating ethical questions, and forming your own evidence-based opinions.

Can I take both PE and Health?

Yes — they're separate subjects covering different ground, so there's no overlap or conflict taking both.

HPE Careers

Both Health and PE are approved University Entrance subjects (see the NCEA & Endorsements section below), and lead into a wide range of study and career paths.

Health & medical

Physiotherapy, nursing, medicine, paramedicine, sports medicine, occupational therapy

Teaching & coaching

PE or Health teaching, primary and early childhood teaching, sports coaching, personal training

Science & performance

Sports science, exercise physiology, strength & conditioning, nutrition and dietetics

People & community

Psychology and counselling, public health and health promotion, sports management and administration, outdoor education and recreation

Fitness-tested careers

Police, fire service, and defence force roles that require a physical fitness standard

Core Concepts

These four ideas show up again and again across Health and PE. They're not just theory: they're the lens you use to explain why something affects wellbeing, not just that it does.

Hauora

Hauora is a Māori view of wellbeing, often explained through Te Whare Tapa Whā — picture a wharenui (meeting house) with four walls, all needed to keep the whole building standing:

Taha tinana (physical wellbeing), taha hinengaro (mental and emotional wellbeing), taha whānau (social wellbeing, your relationships and sense of belonging), and taha wairua (spiritual wellbeing, your sense of identity, purpose, or connection to something bigger than yourself).

When you're analysing an issue, ask: which of these four dimensions does it affect, and how? Strong answers usually touch on more than one.

Socio-ecological perspective

This is the idea that wellbeing isn't just about individual choices — it's shaped by the layers of environment someone lives in: their whānau and friends, their school or workplace, their community, and wider society (laws, policies, media, culture, economics).

Instead of asking "why did this person make that choice?", a socio-ecological lens asks "what wider conditions made this choice more or less likely, or more or less available to them?" It's a key way to move an answer beyond blaming individuals and toward understanding systems.

Health promotion

Health promotion is about improving wellbeing at a population level, not just treating individuals. It's often linked to the Ottawa Charter, which outlines action areas like building healthy public policy, creating supportive environments, strengthening community action, developing personal skills, and reorienting health services.

When an assessment asks about a "strategy" or "intervention," it's usually asking you to think at this bigger-picture level — what could change the environment or system, not just tell individuals to try harder.

Attitudes and values

Values are the beliefs and principles that matter to a person or group — things like fairness, autonomy, tradition, or care for others. Attitudes are the positions people take as a result of those values.

Different people can look at the exact same issue and land in very different places, because they're starting from different values. Being able to explain why a group holds a perspective (not just what they believe) usually means naming the value or belief underneath it.

NCEA & Endorsements

The basics

NCEA is New Zealand's main secondary school qualification, made up of three levels — Level 1, 2, and 3 — usually completed across Years 11, 12, and 13. Within each subject, you're assessed against a number of standards, each worth a number of credits, graded Not Achieved, Achieved, Merit, or Excellence. Standards are either internally assessed (marked by teachers at school) or externally assessed (marked by NZQA, usually an end-of-year exam).

Course (subject) endorsement

Recognises strong performance in one subject, in one year. You generally need 14+ credits at Merit or Excellence (or 14+ at Excellence for an Excellence endorsement), with at least 3 from an external standard and 3 from an internal standard, all in the same school year. It shows next to that subject on your Record of Achievement.

Certificate endorsement

Recognises strong performance across an entire NCEA level: 50+ credits at Merit or Excellence (Merit endorsement), or 50+ at Excellence (Excellence endorsement). This shows on the certificate itself.

Health & PE specifics

Level 2 and 3 PE courses made up entirely of internal standards are exempt from the internal/external split for course endorsement — which matters here, since our Level 3 PE course (3.1, 3.3, 3.4, 3.7, 3.8) is entirely internal. Health doesn't have this exemption, but usually satisfies it naturally via the externally assessed exam standard alongside 3.1, 3.3, and 3.4.

University Entrance

Needs NCEA Level 3 with 14+ credits in each of three approved subjects (Health and PE both qualify), 10 literacy credits at Level 2+ (5 reading, 5 writing), and 10 numeracy credits at Level 1+.

General guides, not official wording. For the definitive, current NCEA rules, the source is nzqa.govt.nz — and always go with what your teacher says if anything here doesn't match what you've been taught.
